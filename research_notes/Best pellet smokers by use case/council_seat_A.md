## 2. Reasoning by use case

**1. Best overall: Weber Searwood 600**
- Why: it is Best Overall at Smoked BBQ Source (Nov 2026) and Taste of Home (Sep 2026), and Bob Vila's searing pick. The measured data supports it: 600°F in 13 min, the controller stays within 5°F, and AmazingRibs measured the grate about 15°F under the set point. AmazingRibs called its smoke "some of the best ... from a pellet burner." Lowe's shows 4.6/5 from 1,820 reviews. Its parent company (Weber Blackstone) is stable.
- Why not the Woodridge Pro: three separate testers found it cannot sear (grates under 300°F at a 500°F setting, and it topped out at 480°F). It also has Traeger's financial risk (revenue down 40% in Q3 2026, ~$400M of notes).
- Strongest argument against: it heats unevenly, 30–50°F from left to right. Its barbecue came out "a tad drier," the wheels are poor, and shelves cost extra. Durability data covers only about 6 months to a year.
- Who should not buy it: people who almost only smoke low-and-slow and want set-and-forget evenness, and people who need more than 420 sq in on the main grate.

**2. Best budget: Pit Boss 850 DX**
- Why: SBS named it Best Budget on its page and in two videos ("the best entry-level pellet grill on the market today"). It has a PID controller (180–500°F), WiFi and Bluetooth, 840 sq in, and Lowe's includes delivery and assembly. Pit Boss offers up to 5 years on parts and electronics.
- Why not the Weber Smoque: it is under $700 only at its $599 sale price (regular $799). It cannot sear directly, its smoke was "not as deep as we would want," and the SBS author would buy the Pit Boss or Bullseye first. It is still the pick for even temperatures: tested left-to-right temperatures were near-identical, it has a 5-year warranty, and its parent company is stable.
- Strongest argument against: I cannot confirm a current price. The 5-year warranty applies to "select" models and is not confirmed for the DX. It is a retailer exclusive, and Pit Boss says it is "refreshing the brand ahead of 2027." It also has a hot spot, a 45-minute app setup and a small sear zone.
- Who should not buy it: anyone who wants a good app, or who wants real searing (look at the recteq Bullseye RT-B380 instead; its price is snippet-only).

**3. Searing: Weber Searwood 600**
- Why: it is the only grill whose sear performance was measured. The grate is open to direct flame across the whole surface, and SBS said its searing was "the best that I've ever tested."
- Why not the recteq X-Fire Pro 825: its 1,250°F figure comes from the manufacturer and nobody instrumented it. The $1,549.99 price is from recteq's site, which the researchers could not fetch. Engadget gave it 84/100 and liked direct-flame finishing. It is the runner-up for buyers who want steakhouse-style crust over versatility.
- Strongest argument against: ~585°F is good browning, not broiler-level heat. Owner reports of "no sear at 500°F" exist, and the right side runs hotter.
- Who should not buy it: buyers who want 800°F+. Go to the X-Fire Pro, or a pellet grill plus a propane sear station.

**4. Large capacity: depends on the sub-case**
- Smoking for a crowd: Grilla Mammoth. SBS rated it Best Large and the best vertical (Nov 2026). It has 1,660 sq in (expandable to 3,300), is double-wall insulated, and was still about half full after 22 hours on a 40 lb hopper. Runner-up: Pit Boss Sportsman 5-Series ($649.99 on Pit Boss's site; 1,679 sq in; 60 lb hopper).
- Grill and smoke for a crowd: Yoder YS1500S ($5,195). It has the largest credible brisket count (6–8), but that count is a dealer claim. Runner-up: LSG 20x42 (1,278 sq in, about 5 briskets per its review).
- Strongest argument against: verticals top out at 350–450°F and cannot sear. Nobody has published rack-to-rack evenness data. The Mammoth's $999 price is not confirmed on Grilla's current site.
- Who should not buy a vertical: a one-grill household. The Searwood XL 600 (630 sq in main grate, $1,199 sale) is the compromise.

**5. Portable: depends on the sub-case**
- Camping, RV or tabletop: Traeger Ranger. It is The Barbecue Lab's camping pick. OutdoorGearLab measured 54 lb and 176 sq in, and it scored 9/10 for wind resistance.
- Tailgating: Grilla Chimp 2.0 (Barbecue Lab's pick, 460 sq in). I cannot confirm it is still sold: Scheels' description of Grilla's range omits it, and its price is from the ~May 2026 test.
- No mains power: GMG Trek Prime 2.0. It runs on 12V natively and draws at most 165 W. The caveats are GMG's weak app (2.6 stars), Trustpilot at ~3.1, and hands-on data only for its predecessor.
- Why not the Traeger Tailgater: four roundups name it by default, but in tests it had the lowest cooking score at Bob Vila (3.8), tough pork, a rusted tray, ash fires, and it is "tipsy" on uneven ground.
- Strongest argument against the Ranger: it costs a lot per square inch, scored 3/10 for portability, needs 120V, and its connectivity is unverified.

**6. Cold or windy climates: Lone Star Grillz 20x36**
- Why: it has thick steel (3/16"), a gasketed and flanged door, and a 40 lb hopper. A reviewer and an owner both report it working in cold weather (Wisconsin; Buffalo at 20°F in high wind).
- Why not the Yoder: a western-Kansas Yoder owner says pellet use rises "dramatically" in wind until a blanket goes on. The runner-up is the Traeger Ironwood ($1,799.99). It is the only mid-price grill in the notes whose insulated dual-wall body is confirmed on the maker's page, but nobody has documented the current generation in cold weather, and Traeger carries company risk.
- Strongest argument against: no controlled cold-weather test exists for any model, and all of this rests on 2022–2023 anecdotes. An LSG owner says the FireBoard controller "gets a little glitchy" in the cold. Lead time is 8–14 weeks, and the grill weighs 395 lb.
- Who should not buy it: most people. A competent PID grill plus a blanket and a windbreak is the value answer.

**7. Smart / WiFi / app: Traeger Woodridge Pro**
- Why: Traeger's app has by far the most ratings and the fastest update pace (Live Activities, iPad layout, a WiFi pairing fix in Oct 2026). Recipes send settings to the grill, and the Woodridge supports two wireless probes. The Timberline has the "market-leading app and features," but at $3,499.99 the extra cost is not about the app.
- Why not the Weber Searwood: Weber Connect has good guided cooks but a smaller rating base, weaker graphs and cook history, and no remote start.
- Strongest argument against: the app depends on Traeger's cloud. In 2020 Traeger dropped support for old controller firmware. The company is shrinking and needed a 1-for-50 reverse split to stay listed. BBB complaints from 2026 include Woodridge Pro control panels that would not power on.
- Who should not buy it: data-focused enthusiasts, who would be better served by FireBoard-based controllers (Yoder, LSG), and anyone who wants local or offline control.

**8. Premium "buy once": Yoder YS640S**
- Why: it has a 10-gauge US-made chamber, a transferable 10-year burn-through warranty and 3 years on the controller and igniter. The reviewer has used one for years with no failures, and two outlets name it as their high-end pick (SBS, The Grilling Dad).
- Why not the LSG 20x36: on paper the LSG is stronger (thicker steel, a lifetime chamber warranty, a pellet dump). But its review dates from 2024–2025, its maximum air temperature is 450°F, and the wait is 8–14 weeks. It is a very close runner-up.
- Would have been: the MAK 2 Star (stainless steel), but MAK closed in May 2026 and said nothing about parts or warranty.
- Strongest argument against: the warranty excludes paint and rust, and humid or coastal owners flag rust. It weighs 335 lb or more, has no pellet dump, the SBS unit was supplied free, and Yoder reports "slight delays" on orders.

**9. Smoke flavor: Lone Star Grillz**
- Why: the strongest superlative in the notes ("unrivaled") plus a mechanism for it: a burn pot that mixes hot-burning and smoldering pellets.
- Why not the Yoder: it is also "noticeably smokier than most," and the YS480S is Taste of Home's Best for Smoking. The Yoder arguably has broader agreement across outlets, so it is a near-tie.
- Would have been: the Camp Chef Woodwind Pro, with a Smoke Box for wood chunks at about $1,400. It is sold out at Camp Chef and reported discontinued.
- Strongest argument against: every smoke judgment is subjective. There is no blind multi-grill test, and owners argue over whether LSGs are "really that smoky." The only tasting results are small and confounded.
- Who should not buy it: buyers under $1,000, who should get the Searwood 600 (AmazingRibs praised its smoke quality, and SBS saw more smoke output than the Woodwind Pro), possibly with a smoke tube.

## 3. Claims my picks hinge on that are unverified or weakly sourced in the notes

- Pit Boss 850 DX's current price, its continued availability, and whether the 5-year warranty covers it (Budget).
- Grilla Mammoth's $999 price and current listing, and the Grilla Chimp 2.0 still being sold (Large capacity, Portable).
- recteq X-Fire Pro's 1,250°F and $1,549.99, both manufacturer or snippet figures; recteq.com could not be fetched (Searing runner-up).
- Yoder YS1500S's 6–8 brisket capacity, which is a dealer claim (Large capacity).
- All cold-weather claims, which are anecdotal and mostly from older generations (Cold: LSG, Ironwood).
- LSG's "unrivaled" smoke, which rests on one reviewer plus contested owner posts (Smoke flavor).
- The Traeger Ranger having no WiFi, and its weight (54 lb measured vs 60 lb listed) (Portable).
- Traeger's warranty length (3 years on the general page vs 10 years on product pages) and its long-term cloud support (Smart pick, Ironwood runner-up).
- AmazingRibs' ~585°F grate reading for the Searwood, which is snippet-only (Searing, Overall).

## 4. Where I expect reasonable people to disagree with me

- Smoke flavor: many would pick the Yoder YS640S/YS480S, because two outlets back it against one reviewer for the LSG.
- Budget: some would pick the Weber Smoque on sale for its evenness, warranty and stable parent, or the Traeger Westwood ($699.99) on brand and app. I discount the Westwood because its 450°F cap is unevidenced beyond Bob Vila and it carries Traeger's company risk.
- Searing: buyers who care only about steaks may put the recteq X-Fire Pro first despite the missing measurements.
- Portable: some will keep the Traeger Tailgater as the default because of availability and the four roundup awards.
- Smart: some will disqualify Traeger on financial and cloud risk and choose Weber Connect.
- Premium: the LSG's lifetime warranty and thicker steel could reasonably beat the Yoder.

## 5. Overall confidence: 0.55

The overall, smart and premium picks rest on fairly consistent current hands-on evidence. The budget, portable, cold-weather and smoke picks rest on single sources, unconfirmed prices or availability, or subjective and anecdotal evidence, and major outlets (Wirecutter, Serious Eats, Consumer Reports) were inaccessible.
