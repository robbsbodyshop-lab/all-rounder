# Closest physician match runs Hermes, not Grok Bot

**No project fully matches the request on Grok Bot or OpenMausBot themselves.** As of 15 November 2026, the research found no verifiable physician who has published a specialty-specific personal assistant built on either framework. The best overall fit for the description is the always-on "AI Operations Partner" run by **Dr Chukwuma Onyeije, a board-certified maternal-fetal medicine (MFM) specialist in Atlanta**: a self-hosted Hermes Agent on a rented VPS, reached through Telegram, with 94 skills that include MFM documentation workflows, described in two posts on his Doctors Who Code blog dated 17 May 2026 ([Doctors Who Code](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/ai-operations-partner-hermes-agent-physician-workflows/)). It misses the one criterion the request names: it runs on Hermes Agent, it predates Grok Bot's 11 August 2026 launch, and its skills are not published. On the literal frameworks, the nearest items are a six-bot Grok Bot team for medical-imaging research traced to a hospital radiology department, an anonymous Grok Bot neurology digest, and an interventional cardiologist who, on one researcher's reading of auto-captions, used Grok Bot live on an American Heart Association stage; each fails on physician verification or on being a persistent specialty assistant. Confidence is high that nothing matching is publicly indexed, moderate that Onyeije is the best public fit, and low that he is the specific project the requester has in mind. The likeliest home for that project is an X post, a share link or a closed physician community that this search could not read, and one detail from the requester (specialty, venue or month) would settle the question.

## Both names are literal products launched on 11 August 2026

The research began from the suspicion that "Grok Bot" and "OpenMausBot" were garbled renderings of OpenClaw, whose own history runs Clawdbot (from 25 November 2025), Moltbot (27 January 2026) and OpenClaw (30 January 2026) ([OpenClaw docs](https://docs.openclaw.ai/start/lore)). All five research notes reject that reading. xAI announced Grok Bot on **11 August 2026** as "your team of always-on agents" working from a computer of their own in the cloud ([xAI](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot)). OpenMausBot is Milind Soni's Apache-2.0 repository, created the same day and described as an "Open-source Grok Bot alternative with a virtual machine that bots can use", with about **23,300 stars and 3,200 forks**; each bot can use a cloud Linux desktop, an isolated local VM or the host machine ([GitHub](https://github.com/milind-soni/OpenMausBot); [README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/milind-soni/OpenMausBot/main/README.md)). The requester's phrase "VM agent framework" echoes that tagline almost word for word, and one researcher put the literal reading at roughly 85–90% likely. Both products are newer than the researchers' background knowledge, so every framework fact here rests on pages fetched on 15 November 2026.

Three consequences shape everything that follows. Any matching build is **at most three months old**, so thin documentation is expected rather than suspicious. The natural artifact on these frameworks is a share link of the form x.ai/bot/… or a single Markdown team playbook imported through "Teams → Import", which means repository search under-samples the ecosystem ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/milind-soni/OpenMausBot/main/README.md)). And physician-facing guidance states that nothing reviewed shows Grok Bot is covered under HIPAA and that patient information should stay out of it ([Passive Income MD](https://passiveincomemd.com/blog/tech/what-is-grok-bot-for-physicians/)), so a physician build on the hosted product is likely to be framed as literature, education or administrative work. The word "-style" in the request also keeps the older self-hosted siblings in play, OpenClaw and Hermes Agent, which is where the verifiable physician builds turn out to live.

## On Grok Bot and OpenMausBot, every candidate misses a criterion

The literal-name search was wide. OpenMausBot's six official team playbooks cover SEO, engineering, marketing, lead mining, competitor monitoring and inbox follow-up, with nothing medical ([openmausbot-teams](https://github.com/milind-soni/openmausbot-teams)), and xAI's own Marketplace has no health category ([xAI Marketplace](https://x.ai/bot/marketplace)). The genre exists; its physician equivalent is simply not on any surface the search could read.

## A maternal-fetal medicine specialist's Hermes Agent is the best overall fit

Once "-style" is taken at its word, one candidate satisfies physician, specialty and always-on personal agent together. **Dr Chukwuma Onyeije, MD, FACOG**, holds board certification in obstetrics and gynecology (1999) and maternal-fetal medicine (2002) and practises at Atlanta Perinatal Associates ([Emory Healthcare](https://www.emoryhealthcare.org/providers/chukwuma-onyeije-778536)). His framework is **Hermes Agent, "a self-hosted, multi-model AI system running on a VPS"** with a Telegram bot as front end; the system runs on a Hostinger VPS under Docker and Traefik, uses a Kimi model through OpenRouter with Claude Sonnet 4 as failover, and costs about $89 a month for the server plus about $127 in tokens ([architecture post](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/hermes-agent-production-architecture-healthcare-ai/)). The build carries **94 skills** across clinical, technical, theological, productivity, research and DevOps work. The specialty content is concrete: a "Signout-to-APSO pipeline for inpatient MFM documentation" that turns a voice memo into a sign-out, a patient-friendly explanation, a referral note, an outpatient workup plan and a draft message to the referring provider, plus a skill for concise MFM letters ([Doctors Who Code](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/ai-operations-partner-hermes-agent-physician-workflows/)). He fences clinical data into lanes, works from de-identified scenarios and calls the deployment "not a hospital-grade enterprise system" ([architecture post](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/hermes-agent-production-architecture-healthcare-ai/)).

The shortfalls are specific. Neither post links a repository, template or skill file, so there is nothing to install. A full-text search of all 49 posts he published between 1 August and 14 November 2026, plus the two May posts, returned **zero mentions of Grok Bot, OpenMausBot, MausBot or x.ai** ([RSS feed](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/rss.xml)). His 3 September post lists his harnesses as Claude Code, Codex, Antigravity, OpenClaw, Hermes and Omarchy and speaks of Hermes in the past tense ("experimented") ([The Harness Is the Workplace](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/the-harness-is-the-workplace/)). On 25 October he introduced "my AI agent, Hark", which flagged a failed hosting payment in its morning brief, without naming the framework underneath ([Hark post](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/the-most-important-thing-my-ai-agent-did-was-nothing/)). That Hark is the May Hermes build renamed is an inference, not a documented fact.

Physician builds with public code do exist one framework over, on OpenClaw, and they rank behind Onyeije only because each is narrower or less verifiable.

## The research notes contradict each other on five points

**Nedoszytko's use of Grok Bot is the sharpest conflict.** One researcher read the AHA recording's auto-captions in full and reports that he named Grok Bot alongside Codex and Claude Code, said "this whole framework, this application was actually built by Grok bot", and described the demo as running on a private server that Grok Bot set up; the captions render the name variously as "Rockbot", "Grokbot" and "Clockbot" ([AHA recording](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t46Bdrf1t9k)). A follow-up check reports the claim unverifiable, because neither the AHA session page nor his own site names any tool, and says no recording was found ([AHA session page](https://ahahealthtech.org/sessions/designing-and-deploying-real-world-ai-agents/); [nedoszytko.com](https://nedoszytko.com/index.html)). A third researcher found the same video but could not retrieve its transcript. The caption reading is direct evidence and the failed lookup is a search miss, so this report treats the Grok Bot detail as probable but single-sourced. It does not change the ranking, because a stage build is not a persistent assistant under either account.

**Onyeije is graded "strong" by one note and "partial" by another.** The first counts a comparable framework as meeting the criterion; the second does not. This report sides with partial: strong on physician and specialty, a miss on the named framework. A third note labels him a near-miss, but it is describing a different project, his 10 February 2026 OpenClaw experiment with his own glucose-monitor data ([Doctors Who Code](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/i-built-an-openclaw-agent-to-understand-what-developers-are-actually-doing-not-because-i-needed-one/)), followed two days later by a post-mortem titled "Why I'm Stepping Back from My OpenClaw Experiment" ([blog archive](https://www.doctorswhocode.blog/blog/)).

**The literal-framework rankings differ because each researcher saw a different slice.** Researchers who read the human-facing bot directories concluded they hold no specialty bots, while the researcher who keyword-filtered the 7,858-entry catalog surfaced the psychiatry digest and several clinician-adjacent shares ([awesome-grok-bot](https://github.com/majiayu000/awesome-grok-bot)). MY-SKILLS was graded weak by a researcher who saw only "Maintained by Aitor" and partial by the one who followed the ORCID link. The merged table above is this report's synthesis, not any single note's ranking.

**"Verified" means different things across the notes.** One calls Kavan Thompson a verified physician on the strength of his README, another notes his GitHub profile is blank, and a third declines to verify him at all. The openbot-radiology builder is identified as an IT student by the one researcher who opened his profile ([GitHub profile](https://github.com/aryaniyaps)) and left unconfirmed by another. Yukio and a hackathon chart-review demo called CuraClaw ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RF8fR4V3EN8)) were opened by one researcher and could not be found by another's searches. Credentials for the code-host and share-link candidates are self-asserted in a README or profile; the physicians documented by an institution are Onyeije (Emory provider profile), Nedoszytko (AHA programme) and Chong (Digital Pathology Association programme).

**Framework facts wobble at the edges.** xAI's documentation says bots "share a persistent cloud computer" while trade press says each bot has its own environment ([xAI docs](https://docs.x.ai/grok-bot/overview); [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/orchestration/spacexais-grok-bot-turns-agents-into-persistent-digital-coworkers-that-can-operate-your-apps-for-120-per-month)). Sources name the vendor as both xAI and SpaceXAI. Two notes quote the OpenMausBot README as saying "formerly OpenGrokBot", while a third did not find that line and found separate repositories using the name ([Z4YT0N/OpenGrokBot](https://github.com/Z4YT0N/OpenGrokBot)). None of these alters a candidate's standing.

## The likeliest hiding places were unreadable, and one detail would settle it

The gaps cluster exactly where a three-month-old physician build would live. **X returned HTTP 402**, so no sharer's profile behind a physician-style handle could be opened, and a thread carrying an x.ai/bot link would be missed. LinkedIn, Doximity, Sermo, Discord, Facebook groups and physician Slack workspaces were not searchable, and Reddit and Hacker News were only partially indexed. The Grok-Bot Dr classroom is private, and the paid sections of Pean's posts, including a "brief on my setup", were unreadable. Grok Bot share pages expose only a name, a first name and a description, never the skills or routines inside. GitHub file-level code search was unavailable, so a fork that keeps the upstream name but adds a medical playbook is invisible, as is any private repository or unshared OpenMausBot backup. On the scholarly side, PubMed and Google Scholar were not queried directly and the OpenAlex lookup failed. Three specific leads also remain open: Onyeije's separate build-log site agenticbuildermd.com and his posts before August 2026, the repositories under Nedoszytko's GitHub accounts, and the founder pages behind an orthopaedic-surgery agent called OrthoClaw, built on a secured OpenClaw variant by an organisation that names no surgeon ([OrthoClaw](https://github.com/MAIVAN-ai/OrthoClaw)). No name was checked against a medical licensing register.

Any one of the following from the requester would turn this from a ranking into an answer. The **builder's specialty** would immediately confirm or eliminate every row above. The **venue and month** in which the project was seen, such as an X post, a LinkedIn post, a conference stage, a Substack or a private group, would say which unread surface to open. Knowing whether the builder literally said "Grok Bot" or "OpenMausBot", or whether the requester is describing it by analogy, decides between the two tables. The **form of the share** matters as well: an x.ai/bot link, a GitHub repository, a playbook file or only a demonstration. The assistant's name or the builder's handle would be decisive on its own. Country and language help more than expected, since several of the self-identified physician builders on code hosts work in Malaysia and Taiwan and would be missed by English-only searching.

## Conclusion

The search shows a pattern more useful than its null result: physicians who build persistent assistants for their own specialty have so far chosen self-hosted, model-agnostic frameworks they can lock down, while the hosted Grok Bot attracts physician activity only at the non-clinical edge, in literature digests, article access and career search. A physician-built specialty assistant on Grok Bot would have to be either scrubbed of patient data or kept private, and both outcomes leave little public trace. The veterinary example shows what the public version looks like when the privacy constraint is looser.

Framework labels also age quickly in this population. Onyeije moved from OpenClaw to Hermes to an agent whose framework he no longer names, Pean cycled through five products in eight months, and the surgical oncologist behind claw-ea forked Hermes Agent in May. A match on the person is therefore more durable than a match on the framework. The cheapest next step is to ask the requester for the specialty; the second cheapest is to ask Onyeije what Hark runs on.
